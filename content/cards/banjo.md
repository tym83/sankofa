The banjo is a drum with a neck. Its round body, once a hollowed gourd and later a wooden hoop, is covered with stretched skin like a drumhead, and the strings run over a bridge that rests on the skin. Beside the long strings runs a short one, the thumb or drone string. The oldest way to play is the downstroke, or clawhammer: a finger strikes down and the thumb catches the drone on the way back, so melody and rhythm come out as one pulse for dancing.

The banjo came from Africa. Enslaved Africans in the Caribbean and the American South rebuilt West African gourd lutes into the banjo, a gourd body with a skin head. Its closest known relative is the akonting of the Jola people of Senegambia: a calabash covered with goat skin, a long neck, a short string sounded by the thumb, and the same downward stroke. A watercolour called The Old Plantation, painted in South Carolina around 1785–1790, shows a banjo beside a drum. Then the instrument changed hands: Joel Sweeney, a white Virginian who learned from Black players, helped make the banjo a minstrel stage instrument. Its bright voice later cut through early jazz bands, drove Black and white string bands at Appalachian dances, and rings through bluegrass. Compare [[stono-drum-ban]], [[blues]] and [[work-songs]].
