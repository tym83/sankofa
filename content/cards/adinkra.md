Adinkra are signs of the Akan people of Ghana, each standing for a proverb, a belief or an idea, and printed by hand on cloth. The printer carves the sign in relief on a piece of calabash and makes a dark ink from the bark of the badie tree. A comb dipped in the ink rules a grid across the cloth, and the stamps fill its sections. Adinkra was traditionally associated with Asante mourning and elite use; over time its use broadened to other ranks and festive occasions too.

A bird looking back over its shoulder carries a whole philosophy. Sankofa, an Akan sign stamped on adinkra cloth, literally means "go back and get it": heritage as a tool to build the future. The cloth is old and alive at once. In 1817 Thomas Bowdich, on a mission to the Asante capital, acquired a stamped adinkra cloth that is now in the British Museum, and Ntonso, near Kumasi, still stamps it today. Across the Atlantic, a heart of tacks on a colonial-era coffin lid at New York's African Burial Ground was hailed as Sankofa, a reading now contested. This museum takes its name from the sign.
