Yoruba is tonal: low, mid and high pitch change what a word means. The
hourglass dùndún and the smaller gángan copy those tones. Hide cords join
the two drumheads; squeezed under the arm, they tighten the skins and bend
the pitch, so the drum speaks the melody of speech. A melody without vowels
and consonants is ambiguous, though. In the Congo forest the missionary John
Carrington, who learned the Kele drum language, saw how drummers solve it:
they wrap each word in stock phrases and repeat them. About eight drum words
carry one spoken word. James Gleick opens his history of information with
this, error correction made of skin and wood. Compare the jeli of
[[sunjata]], and the drums the colonies feared, [[stono-drum-ban]].
