A Yoruba drum can speak, because Yoruba is a tonal language: low, mid and high pitch change what a word means, and the drums copy those tones. On the hourglass dùndún, hide cords join the two drumheads; squeezed under the arm, they tighten the skins and bend the pitch, so the drum follows the melody of speech. In the Congo forest the missionary John Carrington learned the Kele drum language and saw what the drummers do: they wrap each word in stock phrases and repeat them. James Gleick opens his history of information with this. Compare the jeli of [[sunjata]], the drum ban of [[stono-drum-ban]], and the [[amen-break]].
