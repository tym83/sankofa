A Yoruba drum can speak, because Yoruba is a tonal language: low, mid and high pitch change what a word means, and the drums copy those tones. On the hourglass dùndún, hide cords join the two drumheads; squeezed under the arm, they tighten the skins and bend the pitch, so the drum follows the melody of speech. The drummer strikes the head with a curved stick. A dùndún ensemble is led by its largest drum, the ìyáàlù or "mother drum", which carries most of the words while smaller drums keep the rhythm. These Yoruba drums imitate the tones and rhythms of speech. What they say is mostly a traditional repertoire, praise poetry (oríkì) and proverbs, greetings and the praise names of important people, heard in the palaces of kings and in civic and religious rites.

In the Congo forest the missionary John Carrington learned the Kele drum language and saw what the drummers do: they wrap each word in stock phrases and repeat them. James Gleick opens his history of information with this. Compare the jeli of [[sunjata|the Epic of Sunjata]], the drum ban of [[stono-drum-ban|the Stono Rebellion and the drum ban]], and the [[amen-break|\"Amen, Brother\" and the Amen break]].
