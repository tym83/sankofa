Samba is a music and a dance at once. Its root, samba de roda, still gathers people in a circle in the Recôncavo of Bahia: participants sing, play, clap and take turns dancing in the middle. It drew on the dances of the region's enslaved Africans.

The song that made samba famous took shape in 1916 at the house of a Bahian woman in Rio, and its authorship is still argued over. Migrants had carried samba de roda south from Bahia, and Bahian "aunts" led the Black neighbourhood around Praça Onze, called Little Africa. The best known, Tia Ciata, born in 1854 and initiated in [[candomble|Candomblé houses of Salvador]], hosted samba and religious gatherings at her house. There Pelo Telefone took shape, and on 27 November 1916 Donga registered it at the National Library. It was the hit of the 1917 carnival and is often called Brazil's first samba, a title scholars dispute. Other regulars said the tune was collective.

From such yards came the samba schools of Rio's Black neighbourhoods, which compete at carnival, and samba became a symbol of Brazil. Compare [[capoeira|Capoeira]] and [[jazz|jazz]].
