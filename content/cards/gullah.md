On the isolated Sea Islands of the Carolinas and Georgia, descendants of
enslaved West and Central Africans made Gullah, a creole spoken nowhere else.
Many called it broken English until the linguist Lorenzo Dow Turner rowed out
with a recorder in 1932–33. His Africanisms in the Gullah Dialect (1949) listed
nearly 4,000 items of African origin, mostly personal names. On 31 July 1933
at Harris Neck, Georgia, Amelia Dawley sang him a five-line song she could not
translate. It was Mende. Decades later researchers found a woman in the village
of Senehun Ngola who knew the same funeral song, and in 1997 Dawley's family went there; the film
The Language You Cry In (1998) tells the story. Since 2006 a federal heritage
corridor protects the culture. Compare [[spirituals]] and [[work-songs]].
