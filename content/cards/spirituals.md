Spirituals are the religious folk songs that enslaved African Americans made in the South, most likely from the late eighteenth century on, out of Bible stories, hymns and African ways of singing. A leader could improvise a verse while the others answered with a refrain. Singers clapped or shuffled round in a ring in the worship called the shout. They sang of Israel led out of Egypt into Canaan, and words could mean two things at once. Frederick Douglass, singing of Canaan while planning an escape, wrote that "the north was our Canaan". Later claims of songs as escape maps are another matter: see [[underground-railroad-myths|the Underground Railroad myths of quilt codes and the Drinking Gourd]].

On 6 October 1871 nine students left Fisk University in Nashville, a failing freedmen's school, carrying the whole treasury for travel. At first they hardly sang spirituals: "We did not dream of ever using them in public," Ella Sheppard recalled, for they "were associated with slavery and the dark past". Audiences asked for them, Sheppard arranged them in four-part harmony, and the tour raised tens of thousands of dollars (figures vary). The songs took them to Queen Victoria.

The call and answer came from [[work-songs|work songs and field hollers]]. [[burleigh-dvorak|Harry T. Burleigh]] contributed to the concert tradition of spirituals. Spirituals also fed the [[blues|blues]] and [[gospel|gospel]].
