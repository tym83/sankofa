Work songs were made to fit the job. Enslaved African Americans, drawing on West and Central African ways of singing, sang them while rowing boats, felling trees and working the fields, and they set the pace of shared labour. In the work songs of the American South one voice sang a line and the crew answered with a refrain, timing the strokes of work. A worker alone sent out a holler instead, a long bending cry. Outsiders often heard contentment in this singing. Frederick Douglass, who had sung to drown his own sorrow, set them right: "Slaves sing most when they are most unhappy."

The form is older than America. In the 1790s a doctor working in Sierra Leone described rowers singing as a soloist and a chorus: one rower sang a couplet, and all joined in the refrain. After slavery the songs followed group labour into the road gang and the prison. In 1947 and 1948 Alan Lomax taped wood-cutting songs and hollers at Parchman Farm, Mississippi: music made to survive the hardest labour.

Call and answer runs on through the [[spirituals]], the [[blues]] and [[gospel]]; compare the [[talking-drums]].
