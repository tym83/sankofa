The Bamum script is a syllabary, a set of signs each standing for a syllable, made for the Bamum language of the kingdom of Bamum in western Cameroon. In its final form, finished around 1910, it has about 80 signs. It was a working script of state. In Foumban, the royal capital, it served for letters, for court cases and records of births, deaths and land sales, for medicine and for a history of the Bamum people, and it was taught in schools the king founded in and around the town.

A king designed a script for his own people: Ibrahim Njoya of Bamum. He began with hundreds of signs for things and actions, roughly 500 by one count, a memory aid more than a script, and then, through six versions, simplified the pictorial signs step by step into a working system of writing. What happened next is contested. According to the Endangered Alphabets Project, the French administration that took over after the First World War destroyed his libraries and press and banned the teaching; other historians find no evidence of a ban.
