From 1896 Ibrahim Njoya, king of Bamum in western Cameroon, apparently
inspired by a dream, began with roughly 500 signs for things and actions,
a memory aid more than a script. Using the rebus principle he simplified it through six stages until, by 1910, it was A-ka-u-ku, a
syllabary of 80 letters. Njoya used it for the kingdom's history, its laws,
a pharmacopoeia and a calendar, and opened schools in Foumban to teach it.
According to the Endangered Alphabets Project, the French administration
that took over after the First World War destroyed his libraries and press
and banned the teaching. Unicode added A-ka-u-ku in 2009 and the earlier
stages in 2010. Compare [[vai-script]], [[nsibidi]] and [[adlam]].
