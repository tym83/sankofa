Elizabeth Key was the daughter of an English planter, Thomas Key, and an
enslaved African woman. Baptised and held far beyond the nine years her
father had set, she sued in Northumberland County. English common law said a
child followed the father, and Christians were not to be enslaved; on 21 July
1656 the county court declared her free. In December 1662 the General
Assembly answered with one line: all children born in the colony "shalbe held
bond or free only according to the condition of the mother". The children of
enslaved women, whatever their fathers, were now property for life. In 1667
the assembly closed the other door: baptism did not free an enslaved person.
Compare [[anthony-johnson]] and [[ashleys-sack]].
