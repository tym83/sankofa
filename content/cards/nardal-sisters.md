From the late 1920s Paulette Nardal and her sisters, students from
Martinique, kept open house on Sundays at 7 rue Hébert in Clamart. Young
Antilleans and Africans such as Léopold Sédar Senghor and Aimé Césaire met
Harlem Renaissance writers passing through: Claude McKay, Alain Locke,
Countee Cullen. In February 1928 Jane Nardal published "L'internationalisme
noir" in La Dépêche africaine, sketching a movement of Black people of every
origin. In November 1931 Paulette and the Haitian Léo Sajous launched La
Revue du monde noir, bilingual in French and English; it lasted six issues
in about six months. Late in life Césaire said he had gone to the salon only once
or twice. Compare [[hurston]] and [[claudia-jones]].
