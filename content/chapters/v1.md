This chapter starts with counting, because counting is how silence breaks. The [[slavevoyages|SlaveVoyages]] database lists the names of almost 100,000 Africans taken off the ships. Not cargo: names.

The trade had partners on every shore, among them [[aro-and-dahomey|the kingdom of Dahomey and the Aro]]. In 1999 the president of Benin, once Dahomey, asked forgiveness for his country's part in it.

One of those taken, or so he wrote, was a boy from Igboland. [[equiano|Olaudah Equiano]] bought his freedom and wrote a bestseller. In 1783 he brought the story of the [[zong|Zong massacre]] to [[granville-sharp|Granville Sharp]].

Abolitionists answered with images. The [[brookes|Brookes print]], a plan of one slave ship, showed bodies packed like goods. The [[wedgwood-medallion|Wedgwood medallion]] asked "Am I Not a Man and a Brother?" and was worn as jewellery: a question people carried on their bodies.

The law answered the other way. In Virginia, [[partus-1662|the 1662 law of partus sequitur ventrem]] made slavery pass from mother to child. [[anthony-johnson|Anthony Johnson]] became a free landowner. Long after the United States banned the trade, the [[clotilda|Clotilda]] landed captives in Alabama in 1860, and the survivors built their own African Town.
