People did not wait to be freed. [[nzinga|Queen Njinga of Ndongo and Matamba]] is remembered for a famous reported episode: according to a later account, she used an attendant as a seat so that she could meet the Portuguese governor at equal height. In the hills of northeast Brazil, [[palmares|Quilombo dos Palmares]] held out for about a century. In Jamaica, the Windward Maroons secured a treaty with the British in 1739, signed by Quao; [[nanny-of-the-maroons|Nanny of the Maroons]] reportedly opposed it.

In 1740 South Carolina restricted enslaved people's use of drums, horns and other loud instruments, penalising owners who permitted them. Read the law itself in [[stono-drum-ban|the card on the Stono Rebellion and the drum ban]].

Then came the [[haitian-revolution|Haitian Revolution]], which founded a nation in 1804. In 1825 France imposed an indemnity on Haiti as the price of recognising an independence it had already declared. Some scholars think news of Haiti shaped Hegel's master and slave; others contest it, and the question is still open.

Words travelled too: [[walkers-appeal|David Walker's Appeal]] was carried south. [[robert-smalls|Robert Smalls]] put on a captain's hat and escaped by steaming a Confederate ship, the Planter, past Fort Sumter. Harriet Tubman's [[combahee-raid|Combahee River Raid]] set out on the night of 1 to 2 June 1863, and by the end of the operation more than 700 people had been carried out of slavery.

Some famous stories are harder to stand behind. The Underground Railroad quilt code is a modern claim without established contemporary documentation; [[underground-railroad-myths|The card on quilt codes and the Drinking Gourd]] sorts what is documented from what is only told. And resistance did not stop at the Atlantic: in Asante, [[yaa-asantewaa|Yaa Asantewaa]] has her own story.
