People did not wait to be freed. [[nzinga|Queen Njinga of Ndongo and Matamba]] sat, the story goes, on the back of an attendant so that she could meet the Portuguese governor eye to eye. In the hills of northeast Brazil, [[palmares|Quilombo dos Palmares]] held out for about a century. In Jamaica, the Windward Maroons secured a treaty with the British in 1739, signed by Quao; [[nanny-of-the-maroons|Nanny of the Maroons]], by tradition, was against it.

In 1740 South Carolina restricted enslaved people's use of drums, horns and other loud instruments, penalising owners who permitted them. Read the law itself in [[stono-drum-ban|the card on the Stono Rebellion and the drum ban]].

Then came the [[haitian-revolution|Haitian Revolution]], which founded a nation in 1804. In 1825 France imposed an indemnity on Haiti as the price of recognising an independence it had already declared.

Words travelled too: [[walkers-appeal|David Walker's Appeal]] was carried south. Here the museum's two copies part ways. In the male copy, [[robert-smalls|Robert Smalls]] puts on a captain's hat and steams a Confederate ship, the Planter, past Fort Sumter to freedom. In the female copy, Harriet Tubman leads [[combahee-raid|the Combahee River Raid]] on the night of 1 to 2 June 1863, and more than 700 people are carried out of slavery.

Some famous stories are harder to stand behind. No document of the time mentions [[underground-railroad-myths|a quilt code that guided fugitives north]]: it is a modern tale. And resistance did not stop at the Atlantic: in Asante, [[yaa-asantewaa|Yaa Asantewaa]] has her own story.
