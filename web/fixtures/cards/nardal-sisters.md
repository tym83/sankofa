In the late 1920s the Nardal sisters from Martinique, students of English and
literature in Paris, held a Sunday salon in their flat in Clamart. Writers from
the Caribbean, Africa and the United States met there; the poets of the
[[harlem-renaissance]] were read and translated. In 1931 Paulette co-founded La
Revue du monde noir, published in French and English. The young Aimé Césaire
and Léopold Sédar Senghor came to the salon, and in the 1930s they launched the
movement called Négritude, claiming Black identity as a source of pride.
Paulette later said the sisters had opened the way and been forgotten because
they were women.
