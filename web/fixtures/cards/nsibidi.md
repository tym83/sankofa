Among the Ejagham, Efik and Igbo of the Cross River region, nsibidi is a set of
hundreds of signs for love, quarrels, courts, and the secrets of the Ekpe
leopard society. They were painted on walls, cut into calabashes, woven into
cloth and drawn in the air. A British officer published a first list in 1909,
but the system is older. Captives from Calabar carried Ekpe to Cuba, where in
1836 they founded the Abakuá society near Havana; its members still trace
chalk signs called anaforuana that scholars connect to nsibidi. Like the
[[talking-drum]], it is a way of writing that does not need paper.
