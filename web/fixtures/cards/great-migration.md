Between about 1910 and 1970 some six million Black Southerners moved to the
cities of the North and West, one family at a time, by train and bus. They left
Jim Crow, lynching and the boll weevil for jobs in steel mills, stockyards and
car plants. The Chicago Defender, edited by Robert Abbott, urged them on, and
porters on the Pullman sleeping cars carried bundles of it into the South. The
move remade American music: the [[blues]] went electric in Chicago, [[jazz]]
moved north, and Harlem became the stage of the [[harlem-renaissance]]. In 1941
Jacob Lawrence told the whole story in sixty painted panels.
